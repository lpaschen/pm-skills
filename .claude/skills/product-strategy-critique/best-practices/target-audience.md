# Target Audience: Best Practices Reference

## What Excellence Looks Like

A strong target audience definition answers these questions with specificity:
- **Who exactly?** Named segments with clear demographics, behaviors, and roles (e.g., "mid-market SaaS founders with 20–200 employees")
- **What do they have in common?** Shared problems, pain points, workflows, or outcomes they're pursuing
- **Why these people and not others?** Clear articulation of why this segment is the best first wedge for the product

Excellence means you can imagine a real person in the segment without guessing. You understand their day-to-day, not just their industry.

### Ideal Audience Definition Includes:

**Behavioral + Demographic specificity.** Demographics alone (age, company size, geography) are insufficient. Pair with behaviors: "They actively search for X," "They're willing to change tools," "They do Y at least twice per week." Behavioral signals reveal who actually has the problem.

**Jobs-to-be-Done framing.** What job is your audience trying to accomplish? What outcome matters to them? "Founders who need to reduce customer acquisition cost by 20%+ in Q2" is stronger than "SaaS founders."

**Internal Consistency Profile (ICP).** If describing B2B, define: decision-maker role, purchase criteria, budget range, company size/revenue thresholds, tech stack compatibility. All attributes should align logically.

## Red Flags and Deal-Breakers

### "Everyone" or Overly Broad Segments
Strategies claiming "anyone who uses email" or "all small businesses" are non-strategies. Broad markets are harder to reach, message to, and validate. A strategy without specificity is untestable. Breadth should only increase after proving traction with a narrow wedge.

### Primary vs. Secondary Audiences Conflated
**Primary audience:** The first segment you'll target, where you expect fastest adoption and strongest product-market fit.

**Secondary audiences:** Groups who benefit but aren't the initial focus.

Conflating them signals unclear prioritization. A strategy naming five equally weighted audiences has no focus and will spread resources too thin. Prioritization reveals strategic thinking.

### Multiple Conflicting Segments Without Coherence
If the strategy lists "enterprise security teams" and "individual freelancers" as co-primary audiences without explaining how the product serves both or sequencing attack, it's unfocused. These segments have different buying processes, price sensitivity, and feature priorities. Conflicting audiences often signal the product is unfocused or that research is shallow.

### Demographic Definitions Without Behavioral Insight
"Tech-forward professionals aged 28–42 with $100k+ salaries" might be tempting, but lacks insight. Are they frustrated with current tools? Do they have budget authority? Will they actually try something new? Demographics describe who exists; behavior describes who has the problem.

### Aspirational vs. Actual Audiences
A product claiming to target "design-forward CTOs at AI companies" when evidence shows strongest traction with "ops managers at logistics firms" is aspirational, not real. Good audience definitions are grounded in validation data, not founder wishful thinking.

### Audience Too Narrow
If the addressable market becomes vanishingly small after specific constraints (e.g., "Fortune 500 biotech companies with 50+ PhDs in structural biology"), the strategy needs either justification for that niche focus or recalibration toward a broader primary segment with secondary niches.

## Actionability Test

Ask: Can your GTM team execute against this audience definition?

- Can they find, reach, and identify these people reliably?
- Can they build messaging that resonates with shared problems?
- Can they measure if they're converting this audience?

If the answer to any is "not really," the definition is too vague. If you need a 30-minute conversation to explain who your audience is, it's not specific enough.

## What to Scrutinize

**Problem validation.** Do evidence and user research support that this audience experiences the stated problem? Or is the problem inferred?

**Market size estimates.** Are TAM/SAM/SOM figures justified or pulled from air? Audience sizing grounded in zero-based research beats confident guesses.

**Competitive positioning within segment.** Does the strategy explain why this audience would choose you over incumbents or alternatives? Audience definition without competitive clarity is incomplete.

**Go-to-market viability.** Can you realistically reach and sell to this audience with your channel strategy (sales, product-led growth, partnerships, etc.)? A brilliant audience you can't reach is a planning failure.

## Common Mistakes to Call Out

1. **Persona theater.** Creating detailed fictional personas without grounding in actual user research.
2. **Expanding audience over time without explicit secondary tier.** Strategies should sequence: primary first, secondaries after proof.
3. **Mixing roles and personas.** "Small business owners" mixes role and company size; refine to "founders of bootstrapped agencies" or similar.
4. **Forgetting the "why now."** Does this audience have urgency today? Are they searching for solutions? A perfectly described but unmotivated audience is a non-starter.
5. **Silent shifts mid-strategy.** Introducing new audience segments late in the document without justifying deprioritization of earlier segments.

## Summary

The best audience definitions are narrow enough to be actionable, specific enough to be memorable, and grounded in evidence. They enable sharp decision-making: Which features matter to this group? What channels reach them? What messaging lands? Vague definitions defer all these decisions; sharp definitions make them obvious.
