# Channel Strategy Best Practices: Critical Evaluation Framework

## What Excellence Looks Like

A credible channel strategy is **specific, mechanistic, and resource-bounded**. It names exact channels, explains *how* customers move through them, and maps them to the realities of your budget and team. "Content marketing and sales" is a placeholder, not a strategy. Excellence sounds like: "We'll acquire SMB product managers via LinkedIn-targeted ads ($X/month) promoting webinars on workflow automation, converting 15% to a free trial with avg. onboarding NPS of 8+ driving 35% conversion to paid."

The strategy should tie directly to **where your actual customers live and buy**. This requires data: customer interviews about discovery, field sales notes on deal sources, content analytics on what drives traffic. If you don't have this data, the channel strategy is speculative.

---

## Go-to-Market Motions: When Each Fits

**Product-Led Growth (PLG):** Works when the product is intuitive enough for self-service, the buyer can experience value in minutes, and the use case is bottom-up (end users pushing adoption). Appropriate for tools with <$5K ACV. Risk: assumes virality or frictionless conversion; monitor whether free-to-paid conversion actually sustains growth, or if you're just acquiring free users.

**Sales-Led:** Necessary for enterprise deals ($50K+ ACV), complex workflows, or long procurement cycles. Requires sales infrastructure: credible reps, deal training, competitive battle cards. Appropriate when buyers won't evaluate without human contact.

**Community-Led:** Builds loyalty and organic growth when your audience is passionate and collaborative (developers, designers, data engineers). Works as *acceleration* layer alongside other channels, not primary motion for most businesses.

**Partnerships:** Valid when your product solves a problem within another company's workflow—but partnerships are *slow* (6-18 months to revenue) and require your partner to care about your success. Don't rely on partnerships as primary growth unless you've shipped pilots and seen demand signals.

**Marketplace:** Only viable if customers actively browse your category on that platform, and the take-rate leaves healthy unit economics. Many startups add themselves to app stores and see zero traction.

## Channel-Market Fit: Match Strategy to Product Reality

Not all channels work for all products.

- **Price point:** $29/mo SaaS can't support $5,000/customer sales teams. Must lean PLG or freemium + SMB-targeted content. $200K+ deals demand sales. $2K-$10K pricing allows hybrid (sales + some self-serve).
- **Buyer:** Consumers demand consumer channels (social, search, influencers). Enterprise buyers expect industry events, analyst reports, and rep outreach. Mid-market buys on reputation + peer reviews.
- **Complexity:** Simple tools (password managers, note apps) succeed on virality and consumer channels. Complex software (data pipelines, supply chain platforms) needs consultative sales and technical content.
- **Time-to-value:** If users see ROI in hours, PLG works. If it takes weeks of implementation, you need sales involvement and customer success infrastructure.

**Red flag:** Claiming a marketplace or partnership strategy when your product hasn't achieved product-market fit with direct customers. These channels accelerate existing demand; they don't create demand.

## Growth Loops vs. Funnels: Build Durable Mechanics

A **funnel** is: acquisition → activation → conversion. Once a user drops out, they're gone. Funnels are expensive to sustain because you must keep buying new traffic.

A **growth loop** is: user gets value → user acts in a way that brings in more users → cycle repeats. Examples:
- Referral loops: existing users invite colleagues; invites are frictionless because the inviter already knows value.
- Network effects: value increases as more peers join (Slack, Figma).
- Content loops: user action generates SEO-friendly content that brings organic traffic (Zapier, Stripe docs).

Durable growth strategies have at least one loop. Strategies built only on funnels (paid ads → landing page → conversion) are fragile and require increasing spend to grow. Ask: *Does usage naturally drive acquisition?*

## Realism Check: Resources and Stage

A strategy is credible only if your team can execute it.

- **Pre-launch:** You probably can't execute enterprise sales. Focus on PMF (user interviews, PLG metrics, early adoption channels). Paid channels before PMF waste money.
- **Seed stage (< $2M ARR):** Can typically own 1-2 channels deeply. Hiring sales reps before product-market fit is premature. Content + community often smarter than demand gen.
- **Series A ($2M–$10M):** Now you can hire a small sales team or run serious paid campaigns. Still need strong product growth loops or paid will become inefficient.
- **Series B+:** You have capital to expand into 3-4 channels simultaneously and iterate fast.

**Red flag:** A seed-stage company claiming they'll build a sales team, launch a marketplace partnership, run enterprise events, and execute community marketing. Pick one or two and do them well.

## Warning Signs: Common Strategy Failures

1. **"Build it and they will come"** – No go-to-market plan, assuming product quality alone drives adoption. Requires: how will customers discover you?

2. **Single-channel dependence** – All acquisition from one source (e.g., a single customer referring everyone). Vulnerable to algorithm change, partner churn, or market shift. Diversify.

3. **Channels misaligned with buyer behavior** – Buying B2B software at trade shows when your buyers primarily research via Google. Spending on influencers for enterprise SaaS. Know where your buyers actually look.

4. **Budget mismatch** – Claiming content marketing as primary channel but allocating $5K/month to a market that demands $50K+ spend to compete. Be honest: if you can't fund the channel, don't rely on it.

5. **Virality assumption** – "Our product is so good it'll go viral" without evidence that users organically invite others. Only a handful of products have authentic virality (games, networks). Plan for paid or earned channels instead.

6. **CAC-to-LTV blindness** – A channel that requires $2,000 CAC won't work for a $150/mo product with 3-year LTV of $5,400. The math has to work.

## Customer Acquisition Cost Reality Check

Your channel choices are constrained by unit economics:

- **High ACV ($50K+):** Sales cost is acceptable because one customer funds many months of operation. Invest in sales, events, partnerships.
- **Mid ACV ($2K–$10K):** Hybrid. Qualified inbound (content, paid) + sales for deals. CAC target: 0.5–1.5x monthly subscription.
- **Low ACV ($29–$500/mo):** CAC must be 5–10% of first-year revenue or you can't scale profitably. Paid ads only work at massive scale. Prioritize organic: SEO, word-of-mouth, community.

**Reality check:** If your channel requires $500 CAC but you need $2,000 ACV, you have 4 quarters to retain and expand. Low-touch SaaS at $99/mo with $500 CAC is dead on arrival.

---

## Grounding in Actual Buyer Behavior

The strongest channel strategies answer: *Where do our target customers actually discover products in this category?*

- Interview 10 recent customers: "How did you first hear about us? What alternatives did you consider? Where were you when you decided to look?"
- Map the answers to channels: if 70% came from Google search, invest in SEO and paid search. If 60% heard from a peer, build referral mechanics.
- Test early and measure: don't plan 18 months ahead without data. Run a small paid campaign, email campaign, or partnership and measure actual CAC and conversion.

A strategy grounded in real buyer behavior beats a theoretical one every time.

---

## Summary: Markers of a Strong Strategy

- **Specific:** Names channels and mechanisms, not buzzwords.
- **Matched:** Reflects your product's price, complexity, buyer type, and value delivery.
- **Resourced:** Aligns with team size and funding at your current stage.
- **Loopy:** Includes at least one growth loop; doesn't rely only on paid funnels.
- **Math-checked:** CAC and LTV are compatible.
- **Tested:** Grounded in real customer discovery, not assumption.

A weak strategy has none of these. A great one has all of them, with evidence.
