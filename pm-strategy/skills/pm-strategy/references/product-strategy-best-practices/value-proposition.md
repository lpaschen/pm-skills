# Value Proposition Critique: Best Practices Reference

## What a Strong Value Proposition Looks Like

An excellent value proposition makes three things crystal clear:

1. **Who it's for** – the specific customer segment, not "everyone"
2. **What problem it solves** – the concrete pain point or outcome they care about
3. **Why this product uniquely delivers** – the differentiated reason to choose this over alternatives

Strong value props are specific and outcome-focused. "We help enterprise sales teams close deals 30% faster by automating repetitive admin work" beats "Our platform improves sales productivity." The first tells you exactly who benefits, what changes, and why. The second is a hollow claim.

## Features vs. Benefits: The Critical Difference

**Features** are what the product does. **Benefits** are what that capability does *for the customer's business or life*.

- Feature: "AI-powered email drafting"
- Benefit: "Sales reps spend 3 hours less per week on administrative work, freeing them to focus on relationships"

Value propositions that lead with features are fundamentally weak. They describe the product, not the customer outcome. Even technically impressive features mean nothing if the strategy doesn't connect them to a concrete benefit the target audience values.

Red flag: If your value prop reads like a feature list, it's not a value prop yet.

## Genuine Versus Generic Compelling-ness

A genuinely compelling value proposition passes the "so what?" test repeatedly.

**Generic:** "We're the fastest, easiest platform in our category."
**Challenge:** Why does speed matter here? For whom? Compared to what?

**Compelling:** "Marketing teams manage 40% more campaigns with the same headcount by reducing manual work in our visual editor—no coding needed."
**Why it works:** Specific outcome (40% more campaigns), clear audience (marketing teams), concrete mechanism (visual editor removes coding barrier).

Generic value props use superlatives without grounding: "smarter," "faster," "more powerful." These are adjectives, not evidence. Ask yourself: Can the customer *feel* this benefit in their work? Can you measure it? Can they verify it's real?

## The "So What?" Test

Apply this ruthlessly: For each claim in the value proposition, ask "so what?" until you hit something the customer actually cares about.

- "We have machine learning."
  - So what? *Our system learns from your data.*
  - So what? *You don't have to manually update rules.*
  - So what? *You spend less time maintaining the system and more time on strategic work.*

That final answer is the value prop. Everything before it is implementation detail. If you can't reach a meaningful outcome, the claim doesn't belong in the value proposition.

## Vague Claims Are Red Flags

Avoid unqualified language:
- "Faster" – faster than what? Measured how? By how much?
- "Easier" – easier for whom? Compared to what they do today?
- "Smarter" – smarter in what specific way?
- "Better" – better on which dimension and for which use case?

These words appear in weak strategies constantly because they *sound* compelling but communicate nothing verifiable. A strong value proposition trades vagueness for specificity—always.

## Table Stakes vs. True Differentiators

Many claimed value props are just *table stakes*—the minimum expected in the category. They aren't differentiators; they're admission requirements.

- In video conferencing, reliable call quality is table stakes, not a differentiator.
- In password managers, security is table stakes.
- In project management, storing files is table stakes.

When a strategy claims these as value props, the proposition collapses. Customers expect them. Ask: What can this product do that *most competitors cannot* at all, or do significantly better? That's the differentiator. That's the value prop.

## Tight Coupling to Audience and Problem

The value proposition must be inseparable from the target audience and the specific problem you're solving.

**Weak:** "Our CRM is more powerful and flexible than competitors."
**Stronger:** "For regional real estate agencies managing 50–200 agents across multiple markets, our CRM cuts deal-entry time by 60% by automating property and contact data sync from MLS feeds."

The second version is tighter: it names the audience (regional agencies, 50–200 agents), specifies the problem (deal-entry overhead, data sync across systems), and connects it to a measurable outcome (60% time reduction through a specific mechanism).

Generic value props that could apply to any customer segment lack this specificity. They read as marketing puffery because they *are*.

## Common Failure Modes

**Too Many Value Props:** "We're faster, cheaper, more secure, and easier to use." Pick 1–2 core claims and develop them deeply. Multiple props dilute the message and signal that the team hasn't done the hard work of identifying what *truly* differentiates.

**Unsubstantiated Claims:** "The industry's most advanced AI" without evidence. Claims without data, customer stories, or verifiable proof are promises, not value props.

**Copying Competitor Messaging:** Generic language that could describe any player in the space. If you rewrote it for your top three competitors and it still fits, it's not distinctive.

**Internal-Facing Language:** "Leverages deep learning to optimize" is how engineers talk to each other. Customers care about outcomes: "Cuts training time from weeks to days." Translate technical capability to customer benefit.

**Mismatch to Problem:** Claiming speed matters when the customer's real blocker is compliance and risk. Or claiming ease-of-use when the target buyer needs advanced functionality. The value prop must address the *actual* problem the customer is paying to solve.

---

## Evaluation Checklist

When critiquing a value proposition, demand clarity on:

- Is the target audience specific enough to matter?
- Can you name the problem in a single sentence?
- Is the claimed benefit measurable or observable?
- Does it pass the "so what?" test all the way through?
- Is it table stakes or a genuine differentiator?
- Could a competitor claim the same thing truthfully?
- Would a customer in the target segment nod and say "that's exactly what I need"?

If any answer is unclear or weak, the value proposition needs refinement.
