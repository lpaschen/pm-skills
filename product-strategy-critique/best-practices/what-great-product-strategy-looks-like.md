# What Great Product Strategy Looks Like

## The Core Distinction

A great product strategy is a **reasoned, differentiated path to sustainable growth**. It answers six interconnected questions with clarity, specificity, and internal consistency. A mediocre strategy is vague, contradictory, or treats strategy as a collection of disconnected statements instead of a coherent system.

The best strategies are **defensive**—they explain why the chosen path is right *and* why alternatives were rejected. They don't pretend tradeoffs don't exist; they name them.

---

## The Six Dimensions Must Hang Together

A strong strategy shows how each dimension reinforces the others:

- **Target Audience**: Who specifically will we serve? (Not "everyone.")
- **Problem**: What specific pain point do they face that competitors are underserving?
- **Value Prop**: What distinct outcome do we deliver? (Not feature lists.)
- **Differentiation**: Why can *we* deliver this better? (What structural advantage do we own?)
- **Growth**: How do we acquire customers and expand within them? (Viral? Sales? Marketplace effects?)
- **Monetization**: How do we capture value sustainably? (Price model, unit economics, willingness to pay.)

When one breaks, others crack. A strategy that targets "everyone" will have a weak differentiation story and no clear monetization. A strategy with no growth mechanism is aspirational, not strategic.

---

## Hallmarks of Strategic Clarity

**1. Specificity over generality.**
- Weak: "Empower small businesses to sell online."
- Strong: "Help yoga studio owners in North America add 20% more revenue through direct-to-consumer membership models, without selling inventory."

**2. Tradeoffs are named, not hidden.**
- Strong strategies explicitly say: *We are not building for Fortune 500 enterprise. We will not support high-volume transaction processing in year one. We are prioritizing retention over acquisition.* This is integrity.
- Weak strategies use vague language ("future optionality," "we'll evolve") to avoid admitting they haven't thought through conflicts.

**3. The differentiation claim is defensible.**
- Strong: "We own direct relationships with 15,000 restaurant managers through our loyalty app; competitors built B2B dashboards. That network effect is our moat."
- Weak: "Better UX." (Why? For whom? Compared to what? How sustainable?)

**4. Each dimension points to the same outcome.**
- The target, problem, value prop, differentiation, and growth model all reinforce a single coherent thesis. Change any part, and the story still holds.

## Common Failure Modes (Red Flags)

**Trying to be everything to everyone.**
- Signal: "We serve small and large businesses, consumer and B2B, paid and freemium users."
- Why it fails: You can't have a coherent value prop, growth model, or differentiation strategy for fundamentally different customer segments. You'll be mediocre for all.

**Vague language masking lack of thinking.**
- Phrases that should trigger skepticism: "leverage," "synergy," "best-in-class," "world-class," "seamless," "cutting-edge."
- These are content-free adjectives. What specifically are you claiming?

**Confusing tactics with strategy.**
- Weak: "Our strategy is social media marketing, paid search, and partnerships."
- That's a *go-to-market* plan, not strategy. Strategy is the choice of *who and how*; tactics are *implementation*.
- A strong strategy precedes and constrains which tactics make sense.

**Missing tradeoffs.**
- If the strategy feels like it has no downside—"We'll grow fast AND maintain high margins AND serve everyone AND minimize churn"—it's fantasy, not strategy.
- Tradeoffs are where strategic thinking lives.

**Differentiation that is imitable or irrelevant.**
- Weak: "We have better customer service." (Easy to copy. Doesn't drive customers to you.)
- Weak: "We use AI." (Everyone claims this now. What specifically? Why does it matter to the customer?)
- Strong: "Our algorithm is trained on 10 years of proprietary data that competitors can't access, letting us predict inventory needs 2 weeks earlier."

**No clear growth mechanism.**
- If the strategy doesn't explain how you acquire your first 100 customers or how they bring the next 100, it's incomplete.
- Strong strategies explain *why* the value prop is so aligned with customer incentives that growth becomes natural (virality, bottom-up adoption, network effects).

## Strategic Clarity vs. Strategic Mush

**Mush sounds reasonable but dissolves under scrutiny:**
- "We're a platform that empowers creators to reach audiences and monetize their passion."
- Questions that expose the mush:
  - Which creators? (All? Podcasters? Photographers? Writers?)
  - Why would they choose you over YouTube, Patreon, or Substack?
  - How do you make money—take a cut? Ads? Subscriptions? (Answer matters for growth and differentiation.)
  - How do new creators discover the platform? (This isn't explained.)

**Clarity withstands stress-testing:**
- "We're building a marketplace for solo physical therapists to book patients in underserved suburban markets. We charge 15% per booking. Differentiation: we solve the discovery problem (therapists can't market themselves); therapists subsidize user acquisition by recruiting from their existing networks."
- You can poke at this and it holds. Every dimension aligns.

---

## Genuine Differentiation vs. "Me Too"

**True differentiation rests on a defensible advantage:**
- Structural (network effects, proprietary data, regulatory moat, switching costs)
- Founder/team (deep domain expertise, relationships, lived experience)
- Timing (you're early to a shift; competitors are late)

**Me-too claims:**
- "Better product" without saying *how* or *why it lasts*
- "Lower price" when you have no cost advantage
- "More features" when customers don't need them or don't perceive them
- Copying successful competitors without the structural reason they succeeded

**The test:** If a well-funded competitor decided to attack you directly, would your differentiation hold, or would they just outspend you? If it's the latter, you don't have real differentiation.

---

## Summary: What Makes a Strategy Critiqueable

A great strategy is **articulate, defensible, and internally coherent**. It makes specific claims about audience, problem, and differentiation. It names tradeoffs. It explains growth. It's not perfect—it's wrong about specific details—but it's *clearly* wrong, not ambiguously wrong.

When critiquing, look for **internal contradictions** (claims that conflict with each other), **missing dimensions** (no growth story, no defensible differentiation), and **vagueness masking assumptions** (terms that sound meaningful but aren't).

The sharpest strategies read like they could be wrong, but the thinking is sound.
